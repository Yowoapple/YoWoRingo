(Work 02) Research / Method

Beyondthe Point

Estimating earthquake shaking when the rupture has a direction.

The problem

A pointis not a fault.

Early warning systems usually treat an earthquake as a single point, so predicted shaking fades the same way in every direction. That works for small events.

Large, shallow earthquakes are different. The rupture travels along the fault, and it throws stronger shaking ahead of itself. Places in that direction can feel far more than a point-source estimate says. In early warning, that is the worst kind of error: telling people it is weaker than it is.

The method

  1. 01 / Listen

    Watch the shaking fade

    As stations report in, compare how quickly peak ground acceleration drops off toward the east, south, west and north.

  2. 02 / Infer

    Find the direction

    If shaking fades unevenly, the rupture is moving. The imbalance points the way it is travelling.

  3. 03 / Correct

    Raise what lies ahead

    Areas in front of the rupture get their estimated intensity raised. Areas behind it are left alone.

  4. 04 / Update

    Keep listening

    The estimate is not a one-time calculation. It moves up and down every second as new data arrives, and is only pushed to people once it makes sense.

Directivity correction informed by Jan et al. (2018). Station data from the P-Alert network.

What the replay taught me

Direction fixes where the shaking goes, not how big the earthquake is.

In Hualien the first magnitude was close, and correcting direction turned three missed strong-shaking cities into none. In Chishang and Nanxi the first magnitude was off, and correcting direction alone could not close the gap. For Nanxi it even pushed an already high estimate higher. That is the next problem to solve.
